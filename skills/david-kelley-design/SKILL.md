# David Kelley Review (Design Thinking & Innovation)

A critique of a plan, process, guide, deck, feature request or rollout, using design thinking as lenses (the human-centered process associated with David Kelley, IDEO and the Stanford d.school, plus the Double Diamond). The goal is to check whether we are solving the right problem for real users, and whether we tested before committing. Not to validate.

## Trigger

Run only when the user explicitly calls this skill or asks for a "Kelley review", "David Kelley review", or "Design review" by name. Do not apply it to ordinary requests, drafts or plans, even if a critique would seem useful. When it is not invoked, behave normally.

## Mode

- **Review** (default): critique something that already exists.
- **Workshop**: if the user brings a problem with no solution yet (for example "why do customers drop off after signup?"), walk through the stages with them instead: empathize, define, ideate, prototype, test. Go one stage at a time, ask one question at a time, and stop at each stage for their input before moving on.

## Step 1: Confirm what is under review

Identify the subject and who the end user is (customer, end user, buyer, approver, internal team, or whoever uses the output). If either is unclear, ask one question and wait.

## Step 2: Ground it in real context

Before critiquing, check whatever sources of truth the current project or workspace defines (its instructions, files, memory, and any connected tools for support, meetings, tasks or CRM). Use what is there; do not assume any specific document or tool exists. Look especially for real user evidence: support conversations, meeting transcripts, feedback, usage data. If the facts needed are not available, say what is missing and ask. Label anything you cannot verify as an assumption.

## Step 3: Pick the lenses that fit

Choose one to three lenses that genuinely apply. If none fits, say so plainly instead of forcing one.

**1. Empathy: whose problem, seen how?**
- Who is the actual user, and what is their context (role, skill level, device, language, time pressure)?
- Is our picture of them based on observation and their own words, or on what we assume?
- What do they say vs. what do they do? Where is the gap?
- What latent need might they not voice?

**2. Problem framing: are we solving the right problem?**
- Can the problem be stated as one point of view: [user] needs [need] because [insight]?
- Is the framing a disguised solution ("they need a training session", "we need a new feature")?
- Does a "How might we..." question open better options than the current framing?
- Did we explore the problem widely before narrowing to one (the first diamond of the Double Diamond)?

**3. Ideation: did we consider alternatives?**
- Is this the first idea, or one of several?
- What would the opposite, the simplest, or the do-nothing option look like?
- Who outside the team (frontline staff, users, other roles) could have added ideas?

**4. Prototype: what is the cheapest way to learn?**
- Are we building the full thing before learning whether it works?
- What rough version (a sketch, a mock message, a one-page guide, a pilot with one customer) could test it in days?
- What exactly would the prototype need to prove?

**5. Test: did real users touch it?**
- Has anyone from the target group tried it, without us explaining it?
- What would tell us it failed, and when will we check?
- Is there a loop that feeds what we learn back into the next version?

**6. Desirable, feasible, viable**
- Do users want it? Can we deliver it with the people and tools we have? Does it make sense for the business?
- Which of the three is weakest, and is it being ignored?

**7. Journey and handoffs**
- Map the user's steps end to end. Where do they wait, get confused, switch channel or drop off?
- Is the friction in our step, or in a handoff between roles or teams?

## Step 4: Write the critique

Use this shape, kept short:

**The blind spot:** the strongest objection, in one or two sentences.

**Lens:** which lens flags it and why it applies here.

**Evidence:** the facts from the sources behind the critique. Mark assumptions as assumptions.

**Cost of ignoring it:** what happens if nothing changes.

**Smallest test:** one quick, cheap way to check the concern with real users.

**Options:** two or three distinct ways to redirect, one line each, with the main trade-off of each. Do not pick for the user unless asked.

**Question back:** one question that pushes the user's thinking further.

If more than one blind spot is serious, lead with the most important and list the others in one line each at the end.

## Rules

- No praise opener and no softening ("great plan, but..."). Be direct and respectful; critique the idea, not the person.
- Do not roleplay Kelley or quote him. Use the method, not the persona.
- Never invent user research, quotes or data. If there is no user evidence at all, that is itself a finding.
- If a Jobs to Be Done or similar review skill is available, point to it instead of repeating that lens here.
- Stay concise. A sharp critique beats a long one.
- Reply in the language the user writes in.
- The current project's or workspace's own rules (approval gates, confidentiality, tone) still apply.
