# Alex Hormozi Review (Value Pricing & Offer Structuring)

A critique of pricing strategy, offer architecture, perceived value, and commercial packaging using Alex Hormozi's Value Equation and Grand Slam Offer principles from "$100M Offers". The goal is to disconnect price from input costs or hours, maximize perceived value, and eliminate customer hesitation without discounting.

## Trigger

Run only when the user explicitly calls this skill or asks for a "Hormozi review", "Alex Hormozi review", "Value pricing review", or "Pricing review" by name. Do not apply it to ordinary pricing questions or budget reviews. When it is not invoked, behave normally.

## Step 1: Confirm what is under review

Identify the subject: a pricing plan, proposal, tier structure, service bundle, software subscription, or commercial deal. If it is unclear, ask one question and wait.

## Step 2: Ground the critique in real context

Before critiquing, check whatever sources of truth the current project or workspace defines (its pricing model, delivery mechanism, client onboarding time, guarantees, and current price points). Do not invent client revenue numbers or cost savings. If the target client's willingness to pay or economic upside is not stated, label anything you cannot verify as an assumption.

## Step 3: Pick the lenses that fit

Choose one to three lenses that genuinely apply. If none fits, say so plainly instead of forcing one.

**1. The Value Equation (The Perceived Value Audit)**
$$\text{Value} = \frac{\text{Dream Outcome} \times \text{Perceived Likelihood of Achievement}}{\text{Time Delay} \times \text{Effort \& Sacrifice}}$$
- Are we selling the inputs (code, hours, features) or the Dream Outcome (revenue increased, headaches erased, free weekends)?
- What evidence or proof makes the customer believe this will actually work for *them* (perceived likelihood)?
- How much friction, onboarding headache, staff retraining, or setup work are we forcing the customer to endure (effort & sacrifice)?
- How long between them giving us money and them seeing the first tangible result (time delay)?

**2. The Cost-Plus Pricing Trap vs. Value Disconnect**
- Are we pricing based on our hours/server costs ("it took 10 hours, so I charge \$500"), or on the financial value delivered to the buyer?
- If this tool saves or makes the client \$2,000/month, why are we charging a trivial one-off fee instead of capturing a fair share of the value created?
- Does our price point trigger suspicion ("if it's only \$50, it must be fragile or low quality")?

**3. Risk Reversal & Guarantees**
- Who is bearing the risk of the transaction? (If the client pays and it doesn't work, do they eat the loss?)
- What condition-based or unconditional guarantee (e.g., "if your staff doesn't adopt it in 14 days, you pay zero") would eliminate buyer anxiety?
- Is there a clear, high-integrity "reason why" behind our terms that builds trust?

**4. Packaging & Trimming (Bonuses & Speed Multipliers)**
- Are we selling a naked commodity ("an appointment app") instead of a complete turnkey solution ("zero-setup patient booking system with automated SMS reminders and staff training included")?
- What one or two low-cost-to-deliver assets (templates, checklists, white-glove migration) would dramatically increase the perceived value of the offer?

## Step 4: Write the critique

Use this shape, kept short:

**The blind spot:** the strongest commercial objection regarding pricing or offer packaging, in one or two sentences.

**Lens:** which framework flags it and why it applies here.

**Evidence:** the facts from the sources behind the critique. Mark assumptions as assumptions.

**Cost of ignoring it:** what happens if nothing changes (e.g., competing on price against cheap commodities, high sales resistance, low margins).

**Options:** two or three distinct ways to re-anchor the price or repackage the offer, one line each, with the main trade-off of each. Do not pick for the user unless asked.

**Question back:** one question that pushes the user's pricing courage and offer clarity further.

## Rules

- No praise opener and no softening ("great offer, but..."). Be direct and commercial; critique the offer, not the builder.
- Do not roleplay Hormozi or quote him. Use the framework and Value Equation, not the persona.
- Never invent client revenue data or competitor pricing. If data is missing, note it plainly.
- Stay concise. A sharp commercial audit beats a long sales pitch.
- Reply in the language the user writes in.
- The current project's or workspace's own rules (approval gates, confidentiality, tone) still apply.
