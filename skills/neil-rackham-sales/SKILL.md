# Neil Rackham Review (Consultative Sales & SPIN Discovery)

A critique of sales discovery calls, client questionnaires, outreach scripts, and commercial conversations using Neil Rackham's research-backed SPIN Selling methodology. The goal is to stop pitching features prematurely, uncover latent customer dissatisfaction, and guide buyers to articulate the value of solving the problem in their own words.

## Trigger

Run only when the user explicitly calls this skill or asks for a "Rackham review", "Neil Rackham review", "SPIN review", or "Sales review" by name. Do not apply it to ordinary sales copy or general email drafting. When it is not invoked, behave normally.

## Step 1: Confirm what is under review

Identify the subject: an upcoming client discovery agenda, call script, outreach email sequence, meeting notes, qualification checklist, or demo outline. If it is unclear, ask one question and wait.

## Step 2: Ground the critique in real context

Before critiquing, check whatever sources of truth the current project or workspace defines (the client's industry, company size, known operational bottlenecks, tech stack, and the commercial proposal). Do not invent prospect budgets or objections. If the client's current workflow or pain level is unknown, label anything you cannot verify as an assumption.

## Step 3: Pick the lenses that fit

Choose one to three lenses that genuinely apply. If none fits, say so plainly instead of forcing one.

**1. Situation Questions vs. Lazy Interrogation**
- Are we asking the prospect basic background questions that we could have easily discovered with 5 minutes of research beforehand?
- Are we fatiguing the buyer with an interrogation about their setup rather than focusing on their challenges?
- Are Situation questions kept to the bare minimum needed to establish context?

**2. Problem Questions (Uncovering Dissatisfaction)**
- Are we asking questions that invite the prospect to voice specific difficulties, frustrations, or bottlenecks with their current way of doing things?
- Are we assuming they are unhappy with their current tools, or are we helping them express where the system is creaking?
- Does the buyer acknowledge that a problem actually exists, or are we trying to convince them they have one?

**3. Implication Questions (The Cost of Inaction: The Critical Step)**
- Have we explored the downstream consequences, financial waste, time loss, and emotional friction caused by the problem?
- Does the buyer feel the true magnitude and cost of leaving the problem unaddressed ("If this happens 5 times a week, what does that cost you in overtime and lost clients over a quarter?")?
- Are we helping small, annoying problems become urgent, justifiable priorities?

**4. Need-Payoff Questions (Getting the Buyer to Pitch Themselves)**
- Are we telling the buyer how our product solves their problem, or are we asking questions that lead *them* to describe the payoff ("If you could eliminate that manual reconciliation, how would your team spend those 4 hours?")?
- Is the customer building internal conviction by hearing themselves articulate the value of the solution?
- Does the conversation build an internal champion who can defend the purchase to their boss or business partner?

**5. Premature Feature Pitching (The Feature Push Trap)**
- Are we jumping into "demo mode" and showcasing software features before the buyer has felt the severity of the problem?
- Are we talking about our architecture, speed, or tech stack when the buyer is still wondering if they even need to change?

## Step 4: Write the critique

Use this shape, kept short:

**The blind spot:** the strongest objection regarding the sales conversation or discovery flow, in one or two sentences.

**Lens:** which framework flags it and why it applies here.

**Evidence:** the facts from the sources behind the critique. Mark assumptions as assumptions.

**Cost of ignoring it:** what happens if nothing changes (e.g., getting ghosted after sending proposals, "we'll think about it" stalls, client seeing you as a pushy vendor).

**Options:** two or three distinct ways to reframe the questions or redirect the discovery flow, one line each, with the main trade-off of each. Do not pick for the user unless asked.

**Question back:** one question that sharpens the user's discovery inquiry before they talk to the prospect.

## Rules

- No praise opener and no softening ("great pitch, but..."). Be direct and consultative; critique the discovery questions, not the seller.
- Do not roleplay Rackham or quote him. Use the SPIN questioning framework, not the persona.
- Never invent prospect reactions or budget constraints. If evidence is thin, state it as an assumption.
- Stay concise. A sharp sales discovery critique beats a generic sales script.
- Reply in the language the user writes in.
- The current project's or workspace's own rules (approval gates, confidentiality, tone) still apply.
