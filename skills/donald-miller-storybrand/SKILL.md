# Donald Miller Review (StoryBrand Messaging)

Help critique and build marketing and customer messaging using the ideas of "Building a StoryBrand" (Donald Miller): customers do not buy the best product, they buy the one they understand fastest. Clear beats clever. The customer is the hero of the story; the brand is the guide who helps them win. The yardstick is always whether a busy reader understands, in a few seconds, what is offered, how it makes their life better and what to do next.

Scope: the story and clarity of a message. Positioning against competitive alternatives belongs to a positioning lens; the logical structure of an argument (answer first, grouping) belongs to a communication lens; price and offer design belong to a pricing lens.

## Trigger

Run only when the user explicitly calls this skill or asks for a "Miller review", "Donald Miller review", "StoryBrand review", or "Messaging review" by name. Do not apply it to ordinary writing or editing requests, even if marketing is the topic. When it is not invoked, behave normally.

## Step 1: Confirm the subject and the mode

Identify what is on the table: a website or landing page, a pitch or sales deck, an email or sequence, a one-liner or tagline, an offer description, a product announcement, help or onboarding content. Note who the reader is (prospect, existing customer, end user, partner), because the hero changes with the reader.

Then the mode:
- **Review:** critique a message that exists or is drafted.
- **Build:** create a BrandScript (the seven SB7 parts) and a one-liner for an offer, product or audience, or rewrite a piece from it.

If either is unclear, ask one question and wait.

## Step 2: Ground it in real context

Check whatever sources of truth the current project or workspace defines (its instructions, files, memory, existing messaging, and any connected tools for customer conversations, calls or deals). Use what is there; do not assume any specific document or tool exists. Prefer the customer's own words (from calls, chats, emails, reviews) over internal language. If the facts needed are not available, say what is missing and ask. Label anything you cannot verify as an assumption.

## Step 3: Apply the lenses that fit

Choose one to three lenses that genuinely apply. If none fits, say so plainly.

**1. Who is the hero**
- Is the customer the main character, or is the brand talking about itself (history, awards, features, "we" sentences)?
- Count the sentences about the brand versus about the customer's situation.

**2. A clear want**
- Does the message name one specific thing the customer wants, tied to survival (save time or money, reduce risk, gain status, belong)?
- Or does it list many things so nothing stands out?

**3. The problem, on three levels**
- External: the tangible problem.
- Internal: how it makes the customer feel (frustrated, unsure, overwhelmed). This is what people actually buy to resolve.
- Philosophical: why it is wrong that they should have to deal with this.
- Is there a villain (a root cause the customer can point at), or is the problem vague?

**4. The guide: empathy and authority**
- Does the brand show it understands the customer's struggle (empathy)?
- Does it show it can help, briefly (results, customers served, testimonials, credentials), without becoming the hero?

**5. The plan**
- Is there a simple plan of about three steps that makes the decision feel safe (process plan), or promises that reduce fear (agreement plan)?
- Could a reader repeat the steps after one read?

**6. The calls to action**
- Is there one direct call to action, repeated and visible (book a call, start, buy)?
- Is there a transitional call to action for readers not ready yet (a guide, a checklist, a demo video)?

**7. Stakes and success**
- Failure: does it show, without fear-mongering, what it costs to do nothing?
- Success: does it paint a concrete picture of the customer's life after the problem is solved?
- Is there a clear transformation (from who they are to who they want to become)?

**8. Clarity and the grunt test**
- Within a few seconds, can a reader say what is offered, how it makes life better and how to get it?
- Is there jargon, insider language or cleverness that costs clarity?

**9. The one-liner**
- Can the offer be said in one or two sentences: problem, solution, result?
- Does every team member say it the same way?

## Step 4: Write the output

**In Review mode:**

**The confusion:** the biggest reason a reader would not understand or act, in one or two sentences.

**Lens:** which lens flags it and why it applies.

**Evidence:** the lines, sections or patterns behind it, quoted briefly from the user's own material. Mark assumptions.

**Cost of ignoring it:** the likely effect on attention, understanding, replies, sign-ups or sales.

**Options:** two or three distinct ways to fix it, one line each, with the main trade-off. Do not pick for the user unless asked.

**Rewrite:** a short rewrite of the weakest part (a headline, opening, one-liner or call to action) that shows the fix. Keep it in the language and tone of the original.

**Question back:** one question that pushes the thinking further.

**In Build mode:**

**BrandScript:** the seven parts, one or two lines each: character and want; problem (external, internal, philosophical, villain); guide (empathy, authority); plan; calls to action (direct, transitional); failure; success and transformation.

**One-liner:** problem, solution, result, in one or two sentences.

**Built on:** the reader, the customer words and the assumptions it rests on, with assumptions marked.

**Options:** two or three alternative angles (a different want, problem framing or one-liner), one line each, with the trade-off.

**Question back:** one question that would sharpen the message.

If more than one issue is serious, lead with the most important and list the others in one line each at the end.

## Rules

- No praise opener and no softening. Be direct and respectful; critique the message, not the writer.
- Do not roleplay Miller or quote the book. Use the ideas, not the persona.
- Never invent testimonials, results, customer numbers or quotes for the guide's authority. If proof is missing, mark it as a gap and say what proof would fit.
- Clarity over cleverness in every rewrite. No fear-mongering in the stakes.
- Keep it concise and actionable.
- Reply in the language the user writes in; keep customer-facing drafts in the customer's language.
- The current project's or workspace's own rules (approval gates, confidentiality, tone, internal-only information, service levels) still apply. Never put internal targets or internal-only details into customer-facing text. A rewrite or BrandScript is a proposal, never an action taken.
