# April Dunford Review (Positioning)

A critique of product positioning, messaging, category framing, and value articulation using April Dunford's 5-component positioning framework from "Obviously Awesome". The goal is to ensure the product's unique value is instantly obvious to the exact right target customer, not to validate generic marketing copy.

## Trigger

Run only when the user explicitly calls this skill or asks for a "Dunford review", "April Dunford review", or "Positioning review" by name. Do not apply it to ordinary copywriting or drafting requests, even if messaging could be clearer. When it is not invoked, behave normally.

## Step 1: Confirm what is under review

Identify the subject: a landing page draft, product pitch, value proposition, category declaration, sales deck outline, or customer segment description. If it is unclear, ask one question and wait.

## Step 2: Ground the critique in real context

Before critiquing, check whatever sources of truth the current project or workspace defines (its README, codebase, existing customer feedback, pricing, and competing tools). Do not invent customer demographics or market size. If the target audience or alternative solutions are not stated, note what is missing and ask. Label anything you cannot verify as an assumption.

## Step 3: Pick the lenses that fit

Choose one to three lenses that genuinely apply. If none fits, say so plainly instead of forcing one.

**1. Competitive Alternatives (The Real Baseline)**
- If this product did not exist, what would the customer *actually* do? (Excel, pen and paper, hiring an intern, WhatsApp groups, or doing nothing?)
- Are we positioning against direct competitors that customers don't even know, while ignoring the messy manual status quo they currently tolerate?
- Does our messaging explain why we beat the real alternative, or are we fighting phantom rivals?

**2. Unique Capabilities vs. Generic Features**
- What features or attributes do we have that the competitive alternatives genuinely cannot match?
- Are we listing "table stakes" features (e.g., "fast, secure, cloud-based") that every competitor claims, instead of our singular differentiated capability?
- Can a competitor copy-paste our headline onto their site without it being false?

**3. Differentiated Value (Value vs. Feature)**
- What business or personal outcome do our unique capabilities unlock for the customer?
- Are we describing *what the software does* (a feature) instead of *the progress it enables* (the value)?
- Is the value quantified or felt in terms the buyer measures (time saved, revenue recovered, headache eliminated)?

**4. Best-Fit Customer Characteristics**
- Who cares intensely about this specific differentiated value right now?
- Is the target definition too broad (e.g., "small businesses") instead of a sharp profile that has the exact pain point?
- What identifiable attributes make a customer an immediate "hell yes" buyer versus a tyre-kicker?

**5. Market Category & Frame of Reference**
- What context or category are we putting the product in?
- Does our category trigger the right customer expectations regarding price, features, and setup effort, or does it trigger unfavorable comparisons?
- If we call it a "CRM", do they expect Salesforce? If we call it a "Booking assistant", do they expect something lightweight and instant?

## Step 4: Write the critique

Use this shape, kept short:

**The blind spot:** the strongest objection regarding positioning, in one or two sentences.

**Lens:** which framework flags it and why it applies here.

**Evidence:** the facts from the sources behind the critique. Mark assumptions as assumptions.

**Cost of ignoring it:** what happens if nothing changes (e.g., long sales cycles, confusing demo calls, low conversion).

**Options:** two or three distinct ways to reposition, one line each, with the main trade-off of each. Do not pick for the user unless asked.

**Question back:** one question that pushes the user's positioning clarity further.

## Rules

- No praise opener and no softening ("great product, but..."). Be direct and respectful; critique the positioning, not the builder.
- Do not roleplay Dunford or quote her. Use the method, not the persona.
- Never invent buyer data, metrics, or market size. If evidence is thin, say so plainly.
- Stay concise. A sharp positioning critique beats a lengthy essay.
- Reply in the language the user writes in.
